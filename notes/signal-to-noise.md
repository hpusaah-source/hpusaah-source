# Signal-to-Noise

A working note on the operating principle from the Steve Jobs anecdote — what it
actually is, how to run it, and where it breaks.

## The claim

Most people define their priorities on a horizon that is too long to act on.
Vision for the year, goals for the quarter, plan for the week. All of it is real,
and none of it tells you what to do at 9:14 this morning.

The reframe is to shorten the horizon until the answer is forced:

> **Signal is the three-to-five things that must be done in the next 18 hours
> you are awake. Anything that stops you from doing them is noise.**

Not "important" work. Not "high-leverage" work. The things that, if the day ended
with them undone, the day failed. Everything competing with them — however
reasonable, however urgent-feeling, however politely someone asked — is noise by
definition, not by judgment.

That last part is the whole trick. Noise isn't a category of bad things. It's a
category of *everything else*, defined negatively, so you don't have to
re-litigate each interruption on its merits. You already decided.

## Why 18 hours

The horizon is doing real work here, and it's worth being precise about why.

**It's short enough that you can't lie.** A quarterly goal absorbs any amount of
vagueness. "Improve retention" survives ninety days of nothing happening. Three
items due before you sleep either happened or they didn't. The horizon is a
forcing function for specificity: you cannot write an 18-hour item that isn't
concrete, because a vague one is unfinishable and you'd know it immediately.

**It's long enough to hold real work.** An hour is a task. A week is a project.
A day is the largest unit where you still control most of the variables — your
attention, your calendar, your sequence. Past that, other people's decisions
start determining your outcome.

**It resets.** A bad day costs you a day. There's no accumulating debt of
half-finished priorities, no backlog of goals you're quietly behind on. You wake
up and pick three again.

**The cap is the point.** Three to five, not "the top ten." A list of ten
priorities is a list of zero priorities — the ranking never gets tested, because
you never have to *cut* anything. Five is small enough that adding a sixth means
visibly killing one of the first five. That trade is the decision. Anything that
lets you avoid making it is a worse system.

## Running it

**Pick the day's signal before the day picks it for you.** The night before, or
in the first ten minutes. Not after email. Email is a list of other people's
signal, and if you read it first, you'll adopt it.

**Write each item so completion is unarguable.** "Work on the pricing model" is
not an item — you can work on it for six hours and not know whether you're done.
"Send the pricing model to Dana with three scenarios" is. If you can't say what
"done" looks like in a sentence, you haven't finished thinking about it yet, and
that thinking is probably the actual first item.

**Classify interruptions instead of evaluating them.** The question is not "is
this worth doing?" — almost everything is worth doing, which is why that question
never protects you. The question is "is this one of my three?" If it isn't, it
goes in a list for later. Not refused, not judged, just deferred. Most of it
turns out to be genuinely fine to do at 4pm.

**Protect the first block, not the whole day.** Full-day protection fails on
contact with reality. But the first two to three hours are the most defensible
hours you will get, and the highest-value item should live there. Do the hardest
signal item first, while your attention is still yours.

**Score the day, not the week.** Three for three, or two for three. Keep it for a
few weeks. Two patterns will show up fast: the item you keep failing to finish is
mis-scoped, and the thing that keeps eating the day is a structural problem, not
an unlucky one. Both are more useful than the score itself.

## Where it breaks

The framing in the source material is a pitch, and it oversells in three places
worth naming — not to be contrarian, but because the failure modes are what
determine whether this survives past week two.

**"80% signal, 20% noise" is a slogan, not a measurement.** Nobody was
timestamping Steve Jobs' attention. The number is a retrospective
characterization of a man's intensity, told secondhand, decades later, by someone
telling a good story. Treat it as a direction, not a target. If you set out to
hit a literal 80% and measure yourself against it, you will spend a nontrivial
fraction of your signal time measuring your signal time.

**"Zero noise, 24/7" describes no human being.** It's an admiring exaggeration.
The version of this that actually works has a category the framing doesn't
include: work that isn't today's signal and still has to happen. Answering the
person who is blocked on you. Maintenance. Sleep. The relationship you're not
actively spending. Calling all of that "noise" and treating noise as contemptible
is how you get a system that produces three excellent days and a fourth day spent
cleaning up what the first three broke. Noise is *not-today's-signal*, not
garbage. Some of it is load-bearing.

**A short horizon is not a strategy — it's an execution layer.** The 18-hour
frame tells you nothing about whether the three things are the *right* three
things. It is exceptionally good at converting a decision into motion and
completely silent on whether the decision was any good. Something slower has to
choose the direction. Run the daily loop against a direction you revisit weekly
or monthly, or you'll get very efficient at going nowhere, and the efficiency
will feel like progress the entire time.

And one thing that isn't a flaw in the method but is worth separating from it:
the anecdote packages the focus principle with a manager telling a partner to
shut up and do what he says. Those are unrelated. The signal-to-noise discipline
is about what *you* do with *your* attention. Nothing in it requires being
unpleasant to anyone, and the people who cite it to justify that have taken the
easy half.

## The short version

Three to five things, due before you sleep, written so that "done" is
unarguable. Everything else waits. Pick them before the day starts, put the
hardest one first, and re-derive them tomorrow from scratch.

The value isn't the list. It's that having one makes the interruption question
answerable in under a second, several dozen times a day.
