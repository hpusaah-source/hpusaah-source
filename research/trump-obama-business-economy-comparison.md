# Trump vs. Obama: What the Trump Administration Did for Businesses

**A fact-based, source-cited research report**

*Prepared: July 2026 · Scope: Trump first term (2017–2021), compared with the Obama administration (2009–2017), with notes on the second Trump term (2025–present) where data exists.*

---

## How to read this report

You asked a focused question: **Since the Trump administration, what positive things did it bring to the economy — specifically the pros, advantages, and benefits for businesses — compared with the previous (Obama) administration, and what are the cons?** You also asked for the benefits ranked from most to least important, and a final assessment using the **STONE method** (explained in plain English at the end).

Two honesty notes up front, because they govern everything below:

1. **Presidents get too much credit and too much blame.** Much of what happens to the economy is driven by trends, technology, and the business cycle that began before any president took office. Where the evidence says a policy *caused* an outcome, this report says so; where it only *coincided* with one, it says that too.
2. **The two starting points were opposite.** Obama took office in January 2009 in the **worst downturn since the Great Depression**. Trump took office in January 2017 inheriting an **eight-year expansion already underway**. And Trump's first term ended with the **COVID-19 shock**, which wiped out ~15% of US jobs in two months. For a fair policy comparison, this report leans on the **first three years** of each presidency (before COVID distorts Trump's numbers).

## 1. Executive summary

For businesses, the Trump administration's single largest, most durable benefit was the **2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA)**, which permanently cut the corporate tax rate from 35% to 21% and gave pass-through businesses a new 20% deduction. Careful academic research finds it **raised corporate investment in equipment and structures by 8–14%**.[¹](#s1)[²](#s2) Alongside the tax cut, aggressive **deregulation** lowered compliance costs, and **small-business optimism hit an all-time record** in 2018.[³](#s3)[⁴](#s4)

But the benefits were **narrower than advertised**, and they came with real costs for business:

- The tax law **failed at its headline goal** of boosting *aggregate* investment; the non-partisan Congressional Research Service (CRS) found the studies "as a whole … do not demonstrate significant effects of the TCJA on the economy," and there was **no wage surge** for ordinary workers.[⁵](#s5)[⁶](#s6)
- The tax cut **added roughly $1.5–1.9 trillion to federal deficits** over a decade.[⁷](#s7)
- The **2018–2019 trade war was a net negative** for the very manufacturers it was meant to protect (higher input costs + retaliation outweighed import protection), and **trade-policy uncertainty rose nearly fivefold**, denting business investment.[⁸](#s8)[⁹](#s9)
- The **second-term (2025) tariffs** pushed the average US tariff to a **9.6% 80-year high — larger than the 1930 Smoot-Hawley tariffs** — and every credible model projects **lower GDP, investment, employment, and real wages over the coming decade**, with agriculture and durable manufacturing hit hardest.[¹⁰](#s10)[¹¹](#s11)

**Bottom line:** Trump's pro-business agenda delivered a genuine, measurable win on *taxes and deregulation* (good for corporate balance sheets and confidence), largely *continued* an economy that was already strong under Obama, and *actively hurt* business through trade policy. The headline growth and jobs numbers of Trump's first three years were **similar to — and in raw job counts slightly below — Obama's last three years.**

## 2. Side-by-side comparison (the numbers)

These are the metrics you asked for. The underlying data are from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) and Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA); the first-three-years framing is the fairest apples-to-apples comparison because it excludes both Obama's crisis-recovery distortion and Trump's COVID distortion.[¹²](#s12)[¹³](#s13)

| Metric (first 3 full years of each term) | **Obama** (2014–2016) | **Trump** (2017–2019, pre-COVID) | Who looks better |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth (avg annual) | ~2.3–2.4% | ~2.5% | Trump, marginally |
| Net jobs added | **+8.1 million** | +6.55 million | Obama |
| Unemployment rate (start → end) | 6.2% → 4.9% | 4.4% → 3.7% | Trump reached lower low |
| S&P 500 (annualized gain, 3-yr) | ~7.5% | ~12.2% | Trump |
| Wage growth | Steady recovery | Nominal ~3.2% / real ~1.2% (no post-tax-cut surge) | Comparable |

**Key caveats on this table:**
- **Trump inherited a lower unemployment rate**, so the "3.7%" continues a downward line Obama had already established from a 10% post-crisis peak.[¹²](#s12)
- **Obama's raw job creation was higher** in the comparable window (8.1M vs 6.55M), because the recovery was still absorbing slack.[¹²](#s12)
- If you include the **full four years**, Trump's first term shows a **net job loss** due to COVID — the only such record since Herbert Hoover — which is why economists use the pre-pandemic window for policy comparison.[¹³](#s13)
- Stock-market gains are real but reflect many forces (Fed policy, global earnings, tech) beyond any administration.

## 3. The PROS for business, ranked most → least impactful

### #1 — Permanent corporate tax rate cut: 35% → 21% *(biggest, most durable benefit)*

The TCJA **permanently** cut the top federal corporate rate from **35% to 21%**, eliminated the graduated corporate rate schedule, and repealed the corporate Alternative Minimum Tax.[¹](#s1)[¹⁴](#s14) This is the crown jewel of the pro-business agenda because:
- It is **permanent**, unlike most individual provisions that sunset.[¹](#s1)[¹⁵](#s15)
- It took the US from **one of the highest statutory corporate rates in the developed world to below the OECD average**, directly boosting after-tax corporate profits and cash flow.[¹⁴](#s14)

**Why it ranks first:** it is the largest, clearest, and most lasting structural change, and it applies to essentially every C-corporation.

### #2 — A measurable boost to business investment (8–14%) + full expensing

Careful peer-reviewed research (Chodorow-Reich, Smith, Zidar & Zwick) finds the TCJA **raised real corporate investment in equipment and structures by 8–14%**.[¹](#s1)[²](#s2) The law also allowed **100% "bonus" expensing** — letting firms immediately deduct the cost of new equipment. The CBO confirmed the **net effect on investment was positive**.[²](#s2)

**The honest caveat (why it's #2, not #1 in impact):** this was a boost to *corporate* equipment/structures — only about half of *aggregate* investment. On the broader economy-wide measure, the law **did not deliver the promised investment surge** (see Cons).[¹](#s1)[⁵](#s5)

### #3 — The 20% pass-through (QBI) deduction for small and mid-size businesses

For the ~95% of US businesses organized as pass-throughs (sole proprietorships, partnerships, S-corps, LLCs), the new **20% Qualified Business Income deduction** lowered the top effective rate on business income from **37% to 29.6%**.[¹](#s1)[¹⁴](#s14) This directly benefited Main Street businesses, not just large corporations.

**Caveat:** this provision is **temporary** (scheduled to sunset) and its benefits skewed toward higher-income business owners.[¹⁵](#s15)

### #4 — Deregulation: lower compliance costs and business confidence

Executive Order 13771 ("**two-out, one-in**") required agencies to repeal roughly two rules for each new one.[¹⁶](#s16) By one non-partisan accounting (American Action Forum), the Trump administration imposed **~$10 billion/year in net regulatory costs, versus ~$111 billion/year under Obama** — a dramatic slowdown in new regulatory burden.[³](#s3) This lowered compliance costs, especially in **energy, finance, and manufacturing**.

**Caveat:** many deregulations were later challenged in court or reversed, and the *net dollar savings* claimed by the administration are disputed by outside analysts.[¹⁶](#s16)

### #5 — Record small-business optimism and confidence

The **NFIB Small Business Optimism Index hit 108.8 in August 2018 — the highest reading in the survey's 45-year history**, beating the prior record set under Reagan in 1983.[⁴](#s4) 2018 set record-high readings for hiring plans, job openings, and planned compensation among small firms.[⁴](#s4) Confidence itself is an economic input: optimistic owners hire and invest.

**Caveat:** optimism indices are *sentiment*, not *output*; the record confidence did not translate into the economy-wide investment surge that was promised.[⁵](#s5)

### #6 — Energy production and lower input costs

US **crude oil production reached ~13.0 million barrels/day just before COVID — the largest single-term production increase in US history (~2.47M b/d)**.[¹⁷](#s17) Cheap, abundant domestic energy lowered input costs for energy-intensive businesses and made the US the world's top oil producer.

**Big caveat (why it ranks last):** production **also rose sharply under Obama** (US output nearly doubled 2009–2017), and the primary driver in *both* administrations was **hydraulic fracturing technology, not White House policy**.[¹⁷](#s17) This is largely a continued trend, not a Trump-created benefit.

## 4. The CONS for business (vs. the Obama years)

### Con #1 — The trade war hurt the manufacturers it was meant to help

The definitive Federal Reserve study (Flaaen & Pierce) found that tariff-exposed manufacturing industries **experienced *relative reductions* in employment**: a small gain from import protection was **outweighed by higher input costs and retaliatory tariffs**, which also **raised producer prices**.[⁸](#s8) This is the opposite of the stated goal.

### Con #2 — Trade-policy uncertainty deterred investment

Trade-policy uncertainty rose **nearly fivefold** after the 2016 election — the biggest surge since the early-1990s NAFTA debate. Fed economists estimate it **lowered 2018 business investment by ~1% economy-wide and ~4% in manufacturing** (about $32.5B and $22B).[⁹](#s9) Uncertainty is poison for the long-term capital commitments businesses need to make.

### Con #3 — Larger deficits and debt

The TCJA is projected to **add roughly $1.5 trillion (JCT) to $1.9 trillion (CBO conventional) to deficits over its first decade** — closer to **$2.3 trillion including interest**.[⁷](#s7) Rising government debt can crowd out private borrowing and raise long-term interest costs for businesses.

### Con #4 — Specific sectors took direct hits (agriculture, durable manufacturing)

Retaliatory tariffs hammered **US agriculture** so hard that the government paid tens of billions in **farmer bailouts** — the CFR found the great majority of China-tariff revenue was effectively recycled to compensate angry farmers.[¹⁸](#s18) The 2025 tariff round is modeled to hit **agriculture and durable manufacturing hardest**, precisely because they depend on foreign export demand.[¹¹](#s11)

### Con #5 — Second-term tariffs are a historically large tax increase on business inputs

In 2025 the average US tariff jumped from **2.4% to a 9.6% 80-year high — a bigger hike than Smoot-Hawley (1930)**.[¹⁰](#s10) The narrow Section 301/232 tariffs alone are modeled to cut long-run GDP 0.2%, the capital stock 0.1%, and hours worked by **142,000 full-time-equivalent jobs**; every scenario leaves GDP, investment, employment, and real wages **lower over the decade**.[¹⁰](#s10)[¹¹](#s11)[¹⁹](#s19)

## 5. STONE Method Assessment (plain English, step by step)

**"STONE" is a simple 5-step way to weigh evidence and reach an honest conclusion. Each letter is one step:**

> **S** = **State** the question · **T** = **Tabulate** the facts · **O** = **Offset** pros vs. cons · **N** = **Note** the nuance · **E** = **Evaluate** and conclude

Here it is applied to your question:

### **S — State the question (in one sentence)**
*Did the Trump administration's policies make things better for businesses than the Obama administration did?*

### **T — Tabulate the facts (just the evidence, no opinion)**
- Corporate tax rate: cut 35% → 21%, permanently. ✅ *(fact)*
- Business investment (equipment/structures): +8–14% from the tax law. ✅ *(fact)*
- Aggregate investment surge: **did not happen**. ❌ *(fact)*
- Worker wages: **no surge** after the tax cut. ❌ *(fact)*
- GDP growth first 3 yrs: ~2.5% (Trump) vs ~2.3–2.4% (Obama). ≈ *(nearly the same)*
- Jobs first 3 yrs: +6.55M (Trump) vs **+8.1M (Obama)**. ⬇️ *(Obama higher)*
- Deregulation: net new regulatory cost ~$10B/yr (Trump) vs ~$111B/yr (Obama). ✅ *(less burden)*
- Small-business optimism: **record high** in 2018. ✅ *(fact)*
- Trade war: **net job loss** in exposed manufacturing. ❌ *(fact)*
- Deficits: **+$1.5–1.9 trillion** from the tax cut. ❌ *(fact)*

### **O — Offset the pros against the cons**
Line up the wins against the losses:

| **Pros for business** | **Cons for business** |
|---|---|
| Big, permanent corporate tax cut | Trade war raised input costs & cut factory jobs |
| Real (if narrow) investment boost | No promised economy-wide investment surge |
| Pass-through deduction for small firms | Larger deficits/debt (~$1.5–1.9T) |
| Much lighter regulatory burden | Record trade-policy uncertainty deterred investment |
| Record small-business optimism | Agriculture & durable manufacturing hit hard |
| High energy output / low input costs | 2025 tariffs = 80-year-high tax on business inputs |

The **tax and deregulation** column is a genuine, evidence-backed **plus** for business. The **trade** column is a genuine, evidence-backed **minus**. They partly cancel out.

### **N — Note the nuance (the "yes, but…")**
Three things stop this from being a simple scoreboard:
1. **Starting points were opposite.** Obama climbed out of a crater; Trump inherited a healthy economy already growing. Similar growth numbers mean *different* things.
2. **Causation is murky.** The strong 2017–2019 economy largely *continued* the Obama-era expansion; CRS found the tax law and tariffs added little to the *observed* trends. Energy gains came from **fracking technology**, not either president.
3. **Timing matters.** Tax/deregulation benefits were front-loaded and partly temporary; the trade-war costs have grown larger over time, peaking with the record 2025 tariffs.

### **E — Evaluate and conclude**
**For businesses, the Trump administration was a mixed but net-positive story on *domestic* policy (taxes + deregulation) and a net-negative story on *trade* policy — set against an economy that was already strong when he arrived.**

- If your business is a **profitable corporation or a pass-through** that mainly cares about **tax rates and regulatory burden**, the Trump years were **clearly favorable** — lower taxes, lighter rules, record confidence.
- If your business **relies on imported inputs, exports (especially agriculture), or long-horizon capital investment**, the **trade wars and policy uncertainty were a real drag**, and the 2025 tariffs make that worse.
- On the **headline macro numbers you'd read in the news** (GDP, jobs, unemployment), the two administrations are **surprisingly close**, and Obama actually created *more* jobs in the comparable window. The biggest *difference* Trump made was **structural** (the tax code and the regulatory state), not the top-line growth rate.

**In one plain sentence:** *Trump gave businesses a big, lasting tax cut and lighter regulation — a real benefit — but partly undercut it with trade wars and bigger deficits, while riding an economy that was already growing when he took office.*

## 6. Methodology & honesty notes

- **How the facts were gathered:** web-wide search across credible sources, followed by adversarial fact-checking — each key claim was independently challenged by multiple reviewers and only kept if it survived (a 3-vote verification; claims that failed were dropped, e.g., one disputed per-household tariff-cost figure).
- **Source quality:** the load-bearing claims rest on primary/authoritative bodies — **CBO, CRS, the Federal Reserve, BLS, BEA, Brookings, Peterson Institute (PIIE), Tax Foundation, Tax Policy Center, and peer-reviewed academic work (NBER)**. Comparative macro figures in the table are from BLS/BEA data as compiled by mainstream outlets and should be read as *approximate*.
- **Known limitations:** (1) Many 2025 second-term figures are **model projections, not measured outcomes**, and will be revised. (2) Presidential-economy comparisons are inherently imperfect because of differing starting conditions and outside shocks (the Great Recession for Obama; COVID for Trump). (3) This report focuses on *business* effects as requested; distributional and household effects are noted only where they bear on business.
